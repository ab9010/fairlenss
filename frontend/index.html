The problem

What is AI bias, really?

AI bias happens when a model produces systematically different outcomes for different groups of people — not because of anything relevant to the decision, but because of patterns baked into the data it learned from. It rarely looks like an obvious mistake. It looks like a loan approval rate that's quietly ten points lower for one group, or a resume screener that ranks identical qualifications differently.

The unsettling part is that a biased model can still be highly accurate overall. Aggregate accuracy hides group-level unfairness — which is exactly why fairness needs to be measured on its own terms.

Why it matters

AI systems increasingly decide who gets
a loan, a job, or a diagnosis.

The goal isn't just to build better AI. It's to build AI that treats people fairly — and can be responsibly evaluated when it doesn't.

Banking

Loan and credit approval models decide access to housing, education and opportunity.

Employment

Resume-screening tools filter who even reaches a human interviewer.

Healthcare

Risk-prediction models influence who gets prioritized for care.

Criminal justice

Risk-assessment scores can shape bail, sentencing, and parole recommendations.

Housing

Automated screening affects who gets shown or approved for a rental.

Education

Predictive tools are used to flag, track, or evaluate students.

What gets measured

Fairness metrics that speak plainly first, technically second

Every metric on the results dashboard is explained in everyday language before you see the number. "Compare the approval rate between groups" comes before "demographic parity difference."

Demographic Parity

Do different groups get the positive outcome at similar rates?

Disparate Impact

The ratio of the least-favoured group's rate to the most-favoured.

Equal Opportunity

Among people who deserved the outcome, were they identified equally?

Fairness Score

One composite number from 0–100 summarizing the disparity found.
