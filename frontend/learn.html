Understanding AI Ethics

Before you can fix bias, you need language for it.

A working vocabulary for how AI systems can treat people unequally — and where that shows up in the real world.

What is AI ethics?

AI ethics is the practice of designing, deploying, and evaluating AI systems in ways that respect people's rights and wellbeing. It asks practical questions: Who could this system hurt if it's wrong? Who gets to know how a decision was made? Who is accountable when it fails?

What is AI bias?

AI bias is a systematic difference in how a model treats different groups of people, unrelated to anything that should legitimately affect the decision. It typically comes from the data a model learned from, the way the problem was framed, or both.

Why does it matter?

Models that were once research demos now sit inside real decisions — who gets a loan, who gets interviewed, who gets flagged as high-risk. A biased model doesn't just make a technical error; it can compound an existing disadvantage every time it runs.

Where it shows up

Real-world impact

Banking

Loan approval models decide who gets access to credit, housing, and financial opportunity.

Employment

Resume-screening tools decide who a human recruiter even sees.

Healthcare

Risk-prediction models can influence who is prioritized for care or treatment.

Criminal justice

Risk-assessment tools can shape recommendations around bail, sentencing, and parole.

Housing

Automated screening can affect who is shown or approved for a rental application.

Education

Predictive tools are increasingly used to flag, track, or evaluate students.

These categories describe known areas of concern in AI ethics research generally — FairLens does not attribute any specific incident to a named company or product.

Five common patterns

Types of AI bias

1

Dataset bias

The training data itself doesn't accurately reflect the population the model will be used on — skewed samples in, skewed decisions out.

2

Historical bias

Even a perfectly accurate model can reproduce yesterday's inequalities if the historical outcomes it learned from were themselves unequal.

3

Representation bias

Some groups appear so rarely in the data that the model never learns their patterns well, leading to worse predictions for them.

4

Measurement bias

A chosen variable doesn't measure the same thing equally well across groups — e.g. using arrests as a stand-in for "crime committed."

5

Algorithmic bias

Even with balanced data, the model's own objective or optimization choices can systematically favor certain outcomes or groups.

A shared vocabulary

The responsible AI framework

Six principles researchers and regulators commonly return to. Click each one to expand it.

Fairness

Outcomes shouldn't systematically disadvantage a group for reasons unrelated to merit or need.

Transparency

People affected by a decision should be able to understand, in plain terms, how it was made.

Accountability

Someone — a person or organization — must be answerable when an AI system causes harm.

Privacy

Systems should collect and use only the data they genuinely need, and protect it appropriately.

Safety

A system should be tested for how it fails, not just how well it performs on average.

Explainability

It should be possible to trace why a specific prediction or decision came out the way it did.
